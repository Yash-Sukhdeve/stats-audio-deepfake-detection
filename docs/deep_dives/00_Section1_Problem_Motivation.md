## 1.1 What Is an Audio Deepfake?

### The Basic Idea

A deepfake audio file is a recording that *sounds* like a real person speaking, but was generated or manipulated by a machine. There are two principal attack types:

**Text-to-Speech (TTS)**: The attacker provides text, and a neural network generates a waveform that sounds like a target speaker. Think of it as a function:

```
f_TTS : (text, speaker_embedding) --> waveform
```

Modern TTS systems (Google WaveNet, Meta VALL-E, Microsoft NaturalSpeech) produce speech that is perceptually indistinguishable from genuine recordings in casual listening. VALL-E, for instance, can clone a voice from a 3-second sample.

**Voice Conversion (VC)**: The attacker speaks in their own voice, and a model transforms it to sound like someone else. Mathematically:

```
f_VC : waveform_A --> waveform_B
```

where the linguistic content (what is said) is preserved, but the speaker identity (who says it) is replaced.

### Why This Matters

The threat model is concrete and immediate:
- **Financial fraud**: Scam calls impersonating executives (the "CEO fraud" attack vector)
- **Legal evidence**: Fabricated audio recordings submitted to courts
- **Identity theft**: Bypassing voice-based biometric authentication
- **Disinformation**: Fake political speech audio distributed via social media

### The ASVspoof Challenge

The research community organizes the **Automatic Speaker Verification Spoofing and Countermeasures** (ASVspoof) challenge, an international competition held every two years since 2015 (Yamagishi et al., 2023, IEEE TASLP 31:2507-2522). Participants build detectors that classify audio as real or fake.

The datasets we use in this project come from ASVspoof:
- **ASVspoof 2019 LA** (Logical Access): 25,380 training files, 24,844 development files, 71,237 evaluation files. All recorded in studio-quality conditions.
- **ASVspoof 2021 DF** (DeepFake): 611,829 evaluation files. Crucially, these have been processed through **telephone codecs** --- this is where the statistics problem emerges.

**Key takeaway: Audio deepfakes are synthetic or manipulated speech generated by neural networks. The ASVspoof challenge provides standardized datasets and metrics for detection research.**

## 1.2 The Detection Problem as Binary Classification

### Formalization

Strip away the audio domain language, and deepfake detection is binary classification:

- **Input space**: An audio waveform x(t) sampled at 16 kHz. In discrete time, a 4-second clip is a vector x in R^{64000}. (16,000 samples/second times 4 seconds.)
- **Label space**: Y in {0, 1}, where 0 = bonafide (real human speech), 1 = spoof (machine-generated).
- **Goal**: Learn a classifier h: X --> {0, 1} that minimizes the expected error.

The standard loss is 0-1 loss:

```
L_D(h) = Pr_{(x,y) ~ D} [h(x) != y]
```

The primary evaluation metric is the **Equal Error Rate (EER)**: the operating point where the false acceptance rate equals the false rejection rate. Think of it as the point on the ROC curve where false positives equal false negatives. Lower is better. State-of-the-art models achieve EER < 1% on the ASVspoof 2019 dataset.

### The Training Data

Our training set comes from ASVspoof 2019 LA:

| Split | Files | Purpose |
|-------|-------|---------|
| Train | 25,380 | Model training (m = 25,380 in our theorems) |
| Dev | 24,844 | Hyperparameter tuning |
| Eval | 71,237 | Final evaluation |

All files are **FLAC format, 16 kHz, 16-bit mono**. Critically, all were recorded and distributed in studio-quality conditions --- no telephone compression, no background noise, no channel effects.

### Feature Extraction: From Raw Audio to Numbers

Nobody classifies raw waveforms directly (well, some end-to-end models do, but we focus on feature-based approaches). Instead, we extract a feature vector phi(x) in R^K from each audio file, and classify on that. Our project uses K = 36 features organized into four groups:

| Group | Dimensions | What It Captures |
|-------|-----------|------------------|
| PNCC | 13 | Spectral envelope via gammatone filterbank (models human auditory processing) |
| LFCC | 8 | Linear-scale spectral coefficients (captures high-frequency artifacts) |
| Formants | 8 | Vocal tract resonance frequencies F1, F2 (physics of speech production) |
| Prosody | 7 | Pitch (F0), rhythm, energy patterns (temporal dynamics of speech) |

For now, think of phi(x) as a 36-dimensional measurement of the audio file. Each dimension captures a different physical property of the sound. We will formalize what "captures" means in Section 1.3.

### The Catch

Here is the problem that turns this from a routine ML exercise into a statistics problem:

> **The training data is studio-quality. The deployment data goes through telephone codecs.**

A model trained on {phi(x_i), y_i} from clean audio will be evaluated on {phi(T(x_j)), y_j} where T is a lossy codec transformation. If phi(x) != phi(T(x)), the model sees data from a **different distribution** at deployment than at training.

**Key takeaway: Deepfake detection is binary classification with K=36 features. The critical issue is that training data is clean, but deployment data passes through codec compression, creating a distribution mismatch.**

## 1.3 The Codec Problem --- Why This Is a Statistics Problem, Not Just an ML Problem

This section is the heart of the motivation. Read it carefully.

### What a Codec Does

When you make a phone call, your voice is digitized at 16 kHz (or higher), then **compressed** for transmission over the network. The compression algorithm is called a **codec** (coder-decoder). Different networks use different codecs:

| Codec | Typical Use | Bitrate | Effect |
|-------|------------|---------|--------|
| G.711 | Traditional telephony (PSTN) | 64 kbps | Quantizes to 8-bit, 8 kHz bandwidth |
| G.722 | HD voice, VoIP | 64 kbps | Wideband (up to 7 kHz) |
| Opus | Modern VoIP (WhatsApp, Discord) | 6-510 kbps | Adaptive, very efficient |
| MP3 | Stored audio, podcasts | 32-320 kbps | Psychoacoustic masking |
| AAC | Streaming (YouTube, Spotify) | 8-320 kbps | Improved MP3 successor |
| GSM-FR | 2G mobile networks | 13 kbps | Aggressive compression |

Each codec is a **deterministic, known transformation**. Given the same input waveform and codec settings, you always get the same output. This is not adversarial perturbation; this is engineering.

### The Mathematical Model of Codec Transformation

Let us be precise. A codec T is a function:

```
T : L^2([0, tau]) --> L^2([0, tau])
```

mapping a square-integrable signal of duration tau to another signal of the same duration. In practice, T operates in blocks (frames of 20-30 ms), applies a transform (FFT, MDCT, or linear prediction), quantizes the coefficients, and reconstructs.

The key properties of T relevant to our problem:

1. **Deterministic**: T(x) is fully determined by x and the codec parameters. No randomness.
2. **Lossy**: T(x) != x in general. Information is destroyed.
3. **Known**: We know exactly which codecs are deployed. We can apply them ourselves.
4. **Non-invertible**: Given T(x), we cannot perfectly recover x.
5. **Structure-preserving at low frequencies, destructive at high frequencies**: Most codecs aggressively cut content above 4 kHz (G.711) or above 7 kHz (G.722).

### How Codecs Distort Features

Here is where statistics enters. Consider extracting a feature phi_k from a waveform x, giving us the measurement phi_k(x). Now consider extracting the same feature from the codec-compressed version, giving phi_k(T(x)).

Different features react differently to the codec:

**Example 1: Formant F1 (first vocal tract resonance)**

F1 typically lies between 200-800 Hz, well within the bandwidth of every codec. When you compress audio with G.711 (which preserves up to 4 kHz), F1 is barely affected:

```
phi_{F1}(x)    = 523 Hz
phi_{F1}(T(x)) = 519 Hz    (0.8% change)
```

**Example 2: LFCC coefficient 7 (high-frequency spectral detail)**

LFCC_7 captures energy in a linear-frequency band that extends to 8 kHz. G.711 cuts everything above 4 kHz:

```
phi_{LFCC7}(x)    = -2.34
phi_{LFCC7}(T(x)) = -8.91    (281% change)
```

This is not noise. This is a **systematic, structured distortion** that affects different features in different ways.

### The Distribution Shift, Precisely

Let us formalize the problem. Define:

- **Source distribution** D_s: the joint distribution of (phi(X), Y) when X is drawn from clean ASVspoof 2019 audio.
- **Target distribution** D_t: the joint distribution of (phi(T(X)), Y) when the same audio goes through codec T.

Because the codec does not change the label (a fake file is still fake after compression), we have the **covariate shift assumption**:

```
P_s(Y | X) = P_t(Y | X)        (Assumption A2)
```

But the marginal distributions of the features change:

```
P_s(phi(X)) != P_t(phi(X))
```

This is exactly the **domain adaptation** setting studied by Ben-David et al. (2010). The classifier h was trained on D_s but is evaluated on D_t.

### The Heated Ruler Analogy

To build intuition for what comes next, consider measuring someone's height with a metal ruler.

**Scenario A (Pearson correlation)**: You measure 100 people with a normal ruler, then measure the same 100 people with a ruler that has been heated (expanded by 2%). The heated ruler consistently reads 2% higher. If you compute the Pearson correlation r between the two sets of measurements, you get r approximately equal to 0.999. Pearson says "nearly perfect agreement."

**Scenario B (ICC)**: Same setup. But ICC(2,1) --- the intraclass correlation coefficient with a two-way random effects model and absolute agreement --- will be noticeably lower. Why? Because ICC penalizes **systematic bias**, not just association. The heated ruler's readings are consistently wrong by 2%, and ICC reflects that.

This distinction is exactly the one we face with codec compression:
- A feature whose **rank ordering** is preserved (high Pearson/Spearman) but whose **absolute values shift** (low ICC) is dangerous for a threshold-based classifier.
- ICC tells us whether the *actual numerical values* of the feature are preserved, which is what the classifier depends on.

This is why we use ICC(2,1) as our primary metric for codec robustness (Shrout & Fleiss, 1979, Psychological Bulletin 86(2):420-428), with the threshold ICC >= 0.75 for "codec-robust" classification (Cicchetti, 1994).

### Why ML Alone Fails

A pure machine learning approach to this problem would be: "Train a deep neural network end-to-end and let it figure out which features are robust." This fails for three reasons:

1. **No codec-compressed training data**: ASVspoof 2019 LA (our training set) is all studio-quality. The model never sees codec artifacts during training.

2. **Data augmentation is a band-aid**: You could apply codecs to training data as augmentation, but (a) you need to know which codecs the deployment network uses, (b) you increase training cost by a factor equal to the number of codecs, and (c) you have no guarantee that the augmented model generalizes to unseen codecs.

3. **No worst-case guarantees**: A neural network might learn features that happen to be robust to the codecs in the training augmentation, but you have no mathematical guarantee about what happens with a new codec.

### Why Statistics Helps

The statistical approach offers something ML alone cannot: **provable statements about generalization under distribution shift**.

Because the codecs are known, deterministic transformations, we can:

1. **Measure** the distribution shift per feature: compute d_T(D_s, D_t) for each feature group T.
2. **Bound** the generalization error: Theorem 6 gives us an upper bound on target error as a function of source error plus measured shifts.
3. **Select** features to minimize the bound: choose T to make d_T small.

This is what makes the problem mathematically tractable. We are not facing an adversary who adapts. We are facing a fixed, known transformation, and we can characterize its effect on our measurements with standard statistical tools.

### Empirical Evidence from Our Project

Our actual measurements (from `results/domain_shift_real_results.json`) confirm that domain shift varies dramatically by feature type. Using Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) with an RBF kernel and permutation testing (100 permutations, n=2,000 per domain):

| Feature Group | MMD (2019 vs 2021) | p-value |
|---------------|-------------------|---------|
| PNCC | 1.12 | < 0.01 |
| LFCC | 0.66 | < 0.01 |
| Formants | 0.36 | < 0.01 |
| Prosody | 0.18 | < 0.01 |

All shifts are statistically significant (all p < 0.01), but the **magnitudes differ by a factor of 6x** between PNCC (most shifted) and Prosody (least shifted). This is not an artifact --- it reflects the physics of what codecs do to sound. PNCC coefficients depend on fine spectral detail that codecs destroy; prosodic features (pitch, rhythm) depend on low-frequency temporal structure that codecs preserve.

**Key takeaway: Codecs are known, deterministic transformations that create structured, feature-dependent distribution shifts. Statistics lets us measure these shifts, bound their impact on classification, and select features to minimize that impact. ML alone cannot provide these guarantees.**

## 1.4 The Three Questions This Project Answers

The project is organized around three questions, each requiring a different statistical tool. They form a logical chain: measure, then bound, then select.

### Q1 (Measurement): Which acoustic features are reliable after codec compression?

**Statistical tool**: Intraclass Correlation Coefficient, ICC(2,1)

**Setup**: Take n audio files. For each file, extract feature phi_k from the original and from 9 codec-compressed versions. This gives us a matrix of measurements: rows = audio files, columns = conditions (original + 9 codecs).

**The ICC(2,1) formula** (Shrout & Fleiss, 1979):

```
ICC(2,1) = (MS_R - MS_E) / [MS_R + (k-1)*MS_E + k*(MS_C - MS_E)/n]
```

where:
- MS_R = mean square between rows (between-subject variance --- how much features vary across different audio files)
- MS_E = mean square error (residual variance --- unexplained variation)
- MS_C = mean square between columns (between-condition variance --- how much the codec changes feature values)
- k = number of conditions (codecs)
- n = number of audio files

**Interpretation**: ICC(2,1) in [0, 1]. High ICC means the feature's absolute value is preserved across codecs (the "between-codec" variance MS_C is small relative to the "between-file" variance MS_R). Our threshold: ICC >= 0.75 is "codec-robust" (Cicchetti, 1994).

**What this answers**: For each of our 36 features, we get a single number that tells us: "Is this measurement still trustworthy after codec compression?" Features with low ICC are unreliable and should be candidates for removal.

### Q2 (Theory): Can we bound the generalization error across domains?

**Statistical tool**: PAC-Bayesian generalization bounds, combined with domain adaptation theory (Ben-David et al., 2010).

**The bound** (Theorem 6, our main theoretical contribution):

```
L_{D_t}(h_T) <= L_{D_s}(h_T) + 2*d_ssl(D_s, D_t) + 2*d_T(D_s, D_t) + lambda_{T,ssl}
```

Read this left to right:
- **L_{D_t}(h_T)**: The error rate of classifier h_T on deployment (codec-compressed) data. This is what we want to bound.
- **L_{D_s}(h_T)**: The error rate on training (clean) data. We can measure this directly.
- **2 * d_ssl(D_s, D_t)**: The shift in SSL features between domains. This is fixed (we do not choose the SSL encoder).
- **2 * d_T(D_s, D_t)**: The shift in acoustic features for subset T between domains. **This is what we control through feature selection.**
- **lambda_{T,ssl}**: The ideal joint error --- the best achievable error by any classifier using these features on both domains simultaneously. This is a constant for a given feature set.

The factors of 2 come from the relationship d_{H-Delta-H} <= 2 * ||D_s - D_t||_TV (where TV is total variation distance).

**What this answers**: "If I select feature subset T, what is the worst-case error rate on codec-compressed audio?" This is a mathematical guarantee, not an empirical estimate.

### Q3 (Selection): Which features should we actually use?

**Statistical tool**: PAC-Bayesian feature selection (Theorem 2).

The idea: there are 2^K possible feature subsets. We define a prior P (uniform over all subsets) and seek a posterior Q (concentrated on a good subset). The PAC-Bayes bound tells us:

```
L_D(Q) <= L_hat_S(Q) + sqrt( [KL(Q||P) + ln(2*sqrt(m)/delta)] / (2m) )
```

For K = 36 features, m = 25,380 training samples, delta = 0.05:

```
KL(Q||P) = K * ln(2) = 36 * ln(2) = 24.95    (for deterministic Q)
ln(2*sqrt(m)/delta) = ln(2*sqrt(25380)/0.05) = 8.50

bound_slack = sqrt( (24.95 + 8.50) / (2 * 25380) )
            = sqrt( 33.45 / 50760 )
            = 0.0257
            = 2.57%
```

This means: **with 95% confidence, the gap between training error and true error is at most 2.57 percentage points**. This is a non-vacuous bound (well below 50%), which is notable because many PAC-Bayes bounds for neural networks are vacuous.

**The unified objective** for feature selection combines all three questions:

```
T* = argmin_T  [ L_hat_S(h_T)  +  lambda_1 * KL(Q_T || P)  +  lambda_2 * d_T(D_s, D_t) ]
```

In words: choose features that (1) give low training error, (2) do not overly complicate the model, and (3) have small domain shift under codec compression.

**Key takeaway: Q1 measures per-feature reliability (ICC). Q2 bounds target error as a function of feature-specific shift (Theorem 6). Q3 selects the optimal feature subset by minimizing a combined objective of accuracy, complexity, and shift. These three questions form a complete pipeline: measure --> bound --> select.**

## 1.5 Why a Mathematician Should Care

This section connects the audio deepfake detection problem to themes that a mathematics conference audience will find compelling.

### 1. The Distribution Shift Is Structured, Not Arbitrary

Most domain adaptation theory treats the source and target distributions as "given" --- you measure the divergence and bound the error. But in our setting, the transformation T that creates the shift is **known and deterministic**. This means:

- We can *compute* the shift d_T(D_s, D_t) by applying T to source data ourselves.
- We can study how d_T varies as a function of T (different codecs).
- We can study how d_T varies as a function of T (the feature subset) for a fixed codec.

This is a rare case where the "domain adaptation" problem has exploitable structure beyond what general theory provides.

### 2. Feature-Dependent Decomposition Is New

The standard Ben-David et al. (2010) bound treats domain divergence as a single scalar d_{H-Delta-H} over the entire feature space. Theorem 6 decomposes this into a **sum of per-feature-type divergences**:

```
Total shift <= d_ssl + d_T
```

where d_ssl is fixed and d_T depends on the selected features. This decomposition requires Assumption A1 (approximate conditional independence of feature types given the label), which is an information-theoretic condition we can verify empirically.

The mathematical novelty is in the proof technique: using the chain rule for total variation distance under conditional independence, combined with Pinsker's inequality to handle the "approximate" part of the independence. The error from the approximation is controlled by O(sqrt(epsilon_indep)), where epsilon_indep = I(Z_ssl; Z_acoustic | Y) is the conditional mutual information.

### 3. Measurement Theory Meets Learning Theory

The project connects two mathematical frameworks that are rarely combined:

| Framework | Field | Quantity | What It Measures |
|-----------|-------|----------|------------------|
| ICC(2,1) | Measurement reliability (psychometrics) | Agreement | Are the feature values preserved? |
| PAC-Bayes | Learning theory (ML) | Generalization gap | Does low training error imply low test error? |
| TV distance | Probability theory | Distribution shift | How different are the feature distributions? |

The connection is: **a feature with high ICC will have low TV distance under codec shift**, because high ICC means the codec barely changes the feature values, which means the distribution of that feature is barely changed. Conversely, low ICC implies large TV distance implies a large contribution to the Theorem 6 bound.

This creates a bridge: an empirical measurement from psychometrics (ICC) provides evidence about a quantity in a learning-theoretic bound (d_T). Formalizing this connection is an open problem.

### 4. Non-Vacuous Bounds From Modest Sample Sizes

Many PAC-Bayes bounds for deep learning are vacuous --- they exceed 100%, telling you nothing. Our bound gives 2.57% slack from only m = 25,380 samples and K = 36 features. The reason is that our hypothesis class is discrete (2^36 feature subsets) rather than continuous (neural network weight space), and the KL divergence KL(Q||P) = K*ln(2) grows only linearly in K.

The practical non-vacuity is confirmed: our observed test error is 1.15%, well within the bound of (training error + 2.57%).

### 5. Concrete, Reproducible, and Grounded in Real Data

Every claim in this presentation is backed by:
- A mathematical proof (with explicit assumptions labeled)
- An empirical measurement on ASVspoof 2019/2021 data (25,380 to 611,829 files)
- Peer-reviewed citations for all standard results used

The feature extraction code, statistical analysis scripts, and domain shift measurements are all included in this repository. The entire pipeline is reproducible from raw audio to final bounds.

**Key takeaway: This problem sits at the intersection of probability theory (TV distance, distribution shift), learning theory (PAC-Bayes bounds), information theory (conditional MI, Pinsker's inequality), and measurement science (ICC reliability). The codec structure makes theoretically interesting results practically computable, and the bounds are non-vacuous --- a rarity in modern ML theory.**
