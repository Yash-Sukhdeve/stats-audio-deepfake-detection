(1989). \"A concordance correlation coefficient to evaluate reproducibility.\" *Biometrics*, 45(1), 255-268.\n\n**Formula:**\n```\nCCC = (2 * rho * sigma_x * sigma_y) / (sigma_x^2 + sigma_y^2 + (mu_x - mu_y)^2)\n```\nwhere rho is the Pearson correlation, sigma_x, sigma_y are standard deviations, and mu_x, mu_y are means.\n\nEquivalently: CCC = rho * C_b, where C_b is a \"bias correction factor\":\n```\nC_b = 2 / [(sigma_x/sigma_y) + (sigma_y/sigma_x) + ((mu_x - mu_y)^2)/(sigma_x * sigma_y)]\n```\n\n**Relationship to ICC:** For the two-rater case (k=2), CCC is algebraically equivalent to ICC(3,1) (consistency form), NOT ICC(2,1). The connection to ICC(2,1) is approximate but not exact.\n\n**Trade-offs vs. ICC(2,1):**\n- CCC is designed for the **pairwise** case (comparing two methods). You have k=10 conditions. To use CCC, you would need to compute 9 pairwise CCCs (original vs. each codec) and then aggregate (mean? min?). ICC(2,1) handles all k conditions simultaneously in a single omnibus statistic.\n- CCC does not have the ANOVA decomposition that gives you variance components. You lose interpretability.\n- CCC is preferred in method-comparison studies (e.g., \"does a new blood pressure device agree with the gold standard?\") but ICC is standard for multi-rater reliability.\n\n### 4.2 Bland-Altman Analysis\n\n**Reference:** Bland, J.M., & Altman, D.G. (1986). \"Statistical methods for assessing agreement between two methods of clinical measurement.\" *The Lancet*, 327(8476), 307-310.\n\n**Method:**\n1. For each observation i: compute difference d_i = Y_{codec,i} - Y_{original,i}\n2. Compute mean difference (bias): d-bar = mean(d_i)\n3. Compute SD of differences: s_d = std(d_i)\n4. 95% Limits of Agreement (LoA): d-bar +/- 1.96 * s_d\n5. Plot d_i vs. (Y_{codec,i} + Y_{original,i})/2\n\n**What it reveals that ICC does not:**\n- **Proportional bias**: If the bias increases with feature magnitude (visible as a funnel shape in the plot), ICC will not detect this. Bland-Altman will.\n- **Outlier structure**: Individual files with extreme disagreement are visible.\n- **Clinical/practical significance**: You can ask \"Are the 95% LoA within an acceptable range for my classifier?\" This is a domain-specific judgment that a single ICC number cannot capture.\n\n**Trade-offs:**\n- Bland-Altman is **pairwise** (original vs. one codec at a time). For k=10 conditions, you need 9 separate plots.\n- It is **descriptive**, not a single summary statistic. Harder to use as a feature selection criterion.\n- It assumes differences are approximately normally distributed and have constant variance across the range (homoscedasticity). Codec effects may violate this -- narrowband codecs may have larger effects on high-frequency features.\n\n**Your protocol already includes Bland-Altman** (Section 1.5 of your statistical protocol), which is good practice. ICC gives you the decision metric; Bland-Altman gives you diagnostic information about *why* a feature fails.\n\n### 4.3 Coefficient of Repeatability (CR)\n\n**Reference:** British Standards Institution (1979). BS 5497-1: \"Precision of test methods.\"\n\n**Formula:**\n```\nCR = 1.96 * sqrt(2) * s_w\n```\nwhere s_w is the within-subject standard deviation (square root of within-subject variance from one-way ANOVA).\n\nEquivalently, CR = 1.96 * SD(differences) for the two-rater case (which equals the Bland-Altman LoA width).\n\n**Trade-offs:**\n- CR is in the original measurement units (Hz, dB, etc.), so it is directly interpretable: \"F0 values will agree within +/- CR Hz for 95% of measurements.\"\n- It does not produce a unitless coefficient, making cross-feature comparison harder (F0 CR in Hz vs. MFCC_1 CR in arbitrary units).\n- It is a special case of Bland-Altman for the simple repeatability setting.\n\n### 4.4 Summary Comparison Table\n\n| Metric | Handles k>2 | Detects Systematic Bias | Single Number | Unit-Free | Variance Decomposition |\n|--------|------------|------------------------|--------------|-----------|----------------------|\n| ICC(2,1) | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |\n| Lin's CCC | Pairwise only | Yes | Yes | Yes | No |\n| Bland-Altman | Pairwise only | Yes (visual) | No (gives LoA) | No | No |\n| Coeff. of Repeatability | No | No (absorbed into s_w) | Yes | No | No |\n| Pearson r | Pairwise only | **No** | Yes | Yes | No |\n\nFor your use case (multiple codecs, need a single decision criterion, absolute agreement required), ICC(2,1) is the most appropriate choice. The alternatives serve complementary diagnostic roles.\n\n---\n\n## 5. Questions a Mathematician Would Ask (and How to Answer Them)\n\n### Q1: \"What are the distributional assumptions, and are they satisfied?\"\n\n**The assumptions of ICC(2,1):**\n1. **Normality**: r_i ~ N(0, sigma_r^2), c_j ~ N(0, sigma_c^2), e_{ij} ~ N(0, sigma_e^2)\n2. **Independence**: All random effects are mutually independent\n3. **Homoscedasticity**: sigma_e^2 is constant across all (i, j) cells\n4. **Balanced design**: Every subject is rated by every rater (no missing data)\n\n**How to answer:**\n- Normality of the random effects is the weakest assumption. ICC is reasonably robust to non-normality of the marginal distributions (Donner, 1986). What matters is approximate normality of the *residuals*. You can show Q-Q plots of the residuals from the two-way ANOVA.\n- Independence: your files are independently sampled from the ASVspoof dataset, and codecs are applied independently. The assumption is satisfied. **However**, feature dimensions within the same file are NOT independent (e.g., PNCC coefficients 1-13 are correlated). Your implementation pools (file, dimension) pairs as targets. A mathematician might argue this inflates the effective sample size. You should acknowledge this: the pooling treats dimensions as exchangeable, which is an approximation. If challenged, you could compute ICC separately per dimension and report the range.\n- Homoscedasticity: codec effects may be heteroscedastic (G.711 may have larger residual variance than AAC-128k). You can test this with Levene's test on the residuals grouped by codec.\n- Balanced design: Yes, every file is processed through every codec.\n\n**Reference:** Donner, A. (1986). \"A review of inference procedures for the intraclass correlation coefficient in the one-way random effects model.\" *International Statistical Review*, 54(1), 67-82.\n\n### Q2: \"ICC(2,1) can be negative. What does that mean?\"\n\n**Answer:** Yes. ICC(2,1) ranges from a negative lower bound to 1.0. The exact lower bound depends on k:\n\n```\nICC(2,1) >= -1/(k-1)    (approximately, when MS_R < MS_E)\n```\n\nA negative ICC means the between-subject variance is *less* than the within-subject (codec-induced) variance. In practical terms: the codec destroys more information than exists between files. The feature is useless after codec compression -- you cannot distinguish files from each other. Confidence intervals for ICC(2,1) are obtained by inverting this relationship, but the algebra is complex because of the MS_C term. The exact confidence interval for ICC(2,1) involves:\n\n```\nF_L = F / F_{alpha/2, n-1, (n-1)(k-1)}\nF_U = F * F_{alpha/2, (n-1)(k-1), n-1}\n```\n\nthen transforming back to the ICC scale. McGraw & Wong (1996) provide the full derivation.\n\n**Method 2: Bootstrap**\n\nResample files (rows) with replacement, recompute ICC(2,1) for each bootstrap sample, take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. This makes no distributional assumptions. Your pingouin implementation returns confidence intervals -- these use the F-based method.\n\n**Reference:** McGraw, K.O., & Wong, S.P.
