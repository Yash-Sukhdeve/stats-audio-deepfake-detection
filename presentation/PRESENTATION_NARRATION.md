## Slide 2: Understanding the Data (30 seconds)

"Let me start with the data. We used the ASVspoof 2019 dataset — it's an international deepfake detection challenge. About 25,000 audio files. 2,580 are real human speech, 22,800 are fake — generated by 6 different synthesis systems. That's a 1 to 9 ratio."

"From each file, we extract 36 acoustic measurements in four groups: spectral shape, spectral detail, mouth resonances, and speech rhythm."

"Here's the problem: a detector trained on this clean data gets 7% error in the lab. But when the audio goes through phone codecs — which compress the signal — error jumps to 50%. That's a coin flip. The question is: which of these 36 features survive compression AND can still catch fakes?"

## Slide 3: Distributions (30 seconds)

"Before running any statistical tests, we check what the data looks like. These histograms show 12 representative features. Green is real speech, red is fake."

"Most features — 33 out of 36 — are approximately bell-shaped. Three are skewed: pitch variability, one spectral feature, and duration."

"Because some features aren't normally distributed, we decided to use non-parametric tests for everything. Specifically, the Mann-Whitney U test. It works correctly whether the data is normal or not, so we don't lose anything by being safe."

[Click]

## Slide 4: Statistical Tests (30 seconds)

"Now the key question: which features actually differ between real and fake?"

"We ran two tests on every feature. First, the Mann-Whitney U test — it checks if the difference is statistically significant. After correcting for testing 36 features at once using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, 33 out of 36 are significant."

"But with 25,000 files, even a tiny difference is 'significant.' So we also measured Cohen's d — the effect size. This tells us if the difference is big enough to actually matter."

"Look at the chart. Only 4 features — the green bars — have large effects, meaning the gap is more than 0.8 standard deviations. Everything else is small or negligible. And all 4 are formant features — measurements of the first vocal tract resonance."

[Click]

## Slide 5: The 4 Winners (30 seconds)

"Here are the four features that matter."

"F1 bandwidth: real speech averages 515 hertz, plus or minus 87. Fake speech averages 361, plus or minus 120. That's a 154 hertz gap — a large effect. Using just this one number per file, we get 18% error."

"The other three tell a similar story — F1 mean, F1 variability, and the F1-to-F2 ratio."

"We also ran a Kruskal-Wallis test — that's like ANOVA but non-parametric — to check if different types of fakes produce different values. They do. All four features differ significantly across 6 synthesis methods. So these features catch many types of fakes, not just one."

[Click]

## Slide 6: Redundancy (30 seconds)

"But are these four features really different from each other? They all measure the first formant."

"We checked. The correlation matrix shows F1 mean and F1-to-F2 ratio are 91% correlated — they carry almost the same information."

"PCA confirms this. One principal component captures 79% of all four features. And a classifier using just that one component gets 17% error — the same as using all four."

"So these four features are really one signal: the F1 formant characteristics. Real speech has wider, higher, more variable first-formant resonances. Fake speech doesn't. We measure this one pattern in four different ways."

[Click]

---

## Slide 7: Why Formants Differ (30 seconds)

"Why does this difference exist?"

"When you speak, your mouth and throat form a tube. That tube resonates at specific frequencies — those are formants. But the tube also absorbs energy. Tissue, nasal passages, radiation from your lips. This damping makes the resonances wide. About 515 hertz for F1."

"A voice synthesizer doesn't have a physical tube. It generates sound by optimizing a mathematical loss function. This optimization produces spectrally sharp, narrow resonances. About 361 hertz."

"And codecs preserve this difference because they have to. If a codec destroyed the formants, you wouldn't be able to understand speech on the phone. That's why this 154 hertz gap survives compression."

[Click]

---

## Slide 8: Codec Test (30 seconds)

"But how much of the gap does compression actually consume? We tested this directly."

"We took 150 files, compressed each one through 9 different codecs, and measured how much the formant values changed. Same file, before and after — a paired comparison."

"The left chart shows each codec. MP3 at high quality: only 5% of the 154 hertz gap consumed. 8 hertz of change. AAC: 10 to 18%. These survive easily."

"But G.711 — the codec used on telephone lines — consumes 69 to 79% of the gap. That's nearly destroying the signal."

"Honest conclusion: formants work well for social media and streaming. For telephone, they're marginal."

[Click]

## Slide 9: Ablation (30 seconds)

"What happens when you use the wrong features?"

"We tested every possible combination. The left side — green bars — shows formant and prosody features alone. Error stays between 21 and 29% after compression. Stable."

"The right side — red bars — shows what happens when you add PNCC or LFCC features. Error jumps to 42 to 55%. Collapsed."

"The critical finding: PNCC plus formants gives 50% error. Formants alone give 29%. Adding PNCC didn't just fail to help — it actively destroyed the detector. The model learned to rely on fragile features, and when codecs destroyed them, it had nothing left."

[Click]

## Slide 10: Feature Set Comparison (30 seconds)

"Now let's look at actual classifiers. We trained an SVM on different feature sets."

"F1 bandwidth alone — just one number per file — gets 18% in the lab, 24% after codecs. Remarkably good for a single measurement."

"Formants plus prosody — 15 features — gets 11% in the lab, 21% after codecs. The best practical result."

"All 36 features: 9% in the lab — the best lab result — but 55% after codecs. Worse than guessing."

"The best lab performance gives the worst deployed performance. Feature selection isn't optional — it prevents catastrophic failure."

## Slide 11: Classifiers + Collapse (30 seconds)

"We tested five different classifiers on the formant-plus-prosody set. SVM, logistic regression, neural network, decision tree, random forest."

"They all perform similarly — around 11 to 17% in the lab, 21 to 25% after codecs. The features matter more than the model."

"The right panel shows the collapse directly. Blue bars are lab performance, green is after codecs. Formants-plus-prosody degrades by 10 points. All-36 degrades by 46 points — to random chance."

"I'll be honest: 21% error is not competitive with state-of-the-art neural systems that achieve about 2%. But our features are explainable. You can say: this file was flagged because F1 bandwidth is 340 hertz, which is below the natural threshold."

"This difference survives most codecs but is marginal on telephone lines."

"And the most important practical finding: adding fragile features to a detector doesn't just fail to help — it destroys it. Feature selection prevents that collapse."

# Q&A Preparation

## Easy Questions

**Q: "What is a formant?"**
"When you speak, your mouth and throat form a tube — like a bottle. If you blow across a bottle, it resonates at a specific frequency. Your vocal tract does the same thing. F1 is the first resonance — it's mainly controlled by how wide you open your jaw. Typically 300 to 700 hertz."

**Q: "What is a codec?"**
"A codec compresses audio to make it smaller for transmission. G.711 is used on phone lines. MP3 is used for music. AAC is used for streaming. They all throw away some information to reduce file size. The question is whether the information they throw away includes the features we need for detection."

**Q: "What is EER?"**
"Equal Error Rate — it's the point where the false accept rate equals the false reject rate. Lower is better. 50% means you're guessing randomly."

**Q: "What dataset did you use?"**
"ASVspoof 2019 Logical Access — it's the standard international benchmark for audio deepfake detection. 25,380 training files, with fixed train/dev/eval splits defined by the challenge organizers. Every paper in the field uses the same splits."

---

## Medium Questions

**Q: "Why Mann-Whitney and not a t-test?"**
"Three of our features are skewed — pitch variability, one spectral feature, and duration. The Mann-Whitney test is non-parametric, meaning it works on ranks rather than assuming a bell curve. For our 33 near-normal features, both tests give the same answer. But Mann-Whitney is more defensible because it makes no distributional assumptions."

**Q: "What is Benjamini-Hochberg correction?"**
"When you test 36 features simultaneously at a 5% threshold, you expect about 2 false positives by chance. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure controls the false discovery rate — the expected proportion of false positives among your rejections — at 5%. It's less conservative than the Bonferroni correction, which would divide the threshold by 36 and reject almost nothing."

**Q: "What is Cohen's d?"**
"It measures how far apart two groups are in terms of standard deviations. A d of 1.3 means the average real file and the average fake file are 1.3 standard deviations apart on this feature. Below 0.2 is negligible, 0.2 to 0.5 is small, 0.5 to 0.8 is medium, above 0.8 is large. We found only 4 features with large effects."

**Q: "Why is the class ratio 1 to 9?"**
"That's how the ASVspoof challenge was designed — more fakes than real files, reflecting the expected real-world scenario where an attacker generates many fake samples. We handle this imbalance by using balanced class weights in our classifiers, which forces the model to weight real and fake files equally."

**Q: "What is Kruskal-Wallis?"**
"It's the non-parametric equivalent of ANOVA. It tests whether multiple groups have different distributions. We used it to compare the 6 different synthesis systems — A01 through A06 — and found that all 4 winning features produce significantly different values across attack types. This means our features aren't just detecting one specific synthesizer."

---

## Hard Questions

**Q: "33 out of 36 significant — isn't that just because you have 25,000 samples?"**
"Yes, exactly. That's why we report effect sizes alongside p-values. With 25,000 files, a difference of 0.04 hertz in duration is statistically significant at p equals 0.0004. But the Cohen's d is 0.03 — completely negligible. Only 4 features have large effects. The effect size is what tells you it matters, not the p-value."

**Q: "Your 4 features are 91% correlated. Aren't you overfitting to one signal?"**
"We're not overfitting — we're discovering that one signal. The 4 features emerged independently from our analysis. The correlation is a finding, not a design choice. PCA confirms it: one component captures 79%, and a classifier using just that one component achieves the same error rate as all 4. You could honestly present this as: we found one robust acoustic signal — F1 formant characteristics — that detects deepfakes."

**Q: "21% error isn't very good. Why should we care?"**
"Two reasons. First: the all-36-feature detector gets 9% in the lab but 55% after codecs — worse than guessing. Our detector gets 11% in the lab and 21% after codecs. Worse in the lab, far better deployed. Feature selection is the difference between a working system and a broken one."

"Second: our features are explainable. You can tell a non-technical person: this file was flagged because the first resonance of the speaker's voice is 340 hertz wide, and natural speech is typically 430 to 600 hertz wide. A neural network that gets 2% error can't explain its decisions."

**Q: "G.711 consumes 79% of the gap. Doesn't that mean formants don't work on phone calls?"**
"For G.711 specifically, yes, detection degrades significantly. Our 21% overall error rate on the 2021 dataset includes G.711 files — that's part of why it's 21% and not better. For MP3 and AAC — which is what social media platforms use — formants change by only 5 to 18% of the gap and work well. The honest message is: formants are strong for streaming and VoIP, marginal for legacy telephony."

**Q: "Why does adding PNCC destroy the detector?"**
"The SVM uses all input dimensions to draw its decision boundary. When PNCC features are included, the model learns to use them because they provide some separation on the clean training data. But codecs destroy PNCC values. At deployment, those dimensions become noise, and the decision boundary that relied on them becomes meaningless. It's like building a house on sand — looks fine until the tide comes in."

**Q: "Is there selection bias? You selected features on training data and then trained classifiers on the same data."**
"There is a risk. Ambroise and McLachlan showed in 2002 that this can produce 5 to 15% optimistic bias in error estimates. However, our final evaluation uses held-out dev and eval sets that were not used for feature selection. The feature selection decision might be biased, but the reported error rates are from proper held-out evaluation. Ideally, we would use nested cross-validation — that's a limitation we acknowledge."

**Q: "Advanced vocoders can produce realistic formants. Doesn't this make your approach obsolete?"**
"Partially. In our evaluation, attacks A17 through A19 use advanced waveform-level voice conversion, and our formant features fail completely — error above 48%. These systems can mimic physical vocal tract properties. Formant features catch most current TTS and voice conversion systems — 10 out of 13 unseen attacks — but will become less effective as synthesis improves. They should be combined with neural features for a complete system."

**Q: "Why not just use deep learning?"**
"Deep learning systems like wav2vec2 achieve about 2% error on this task. We can't compete on accuracy. But we can compete on explainability. A neural network says 'fake' and nobody knows why. Our system says 'fake because the first formant bandwidth is 340 hertz, which is below the 430 hertz threshold typical of natural speech.' That explanation can be presented as evidence in court, used for regulatory compliance, or understood by a non-technical investigator."

## Practice Tips

1. **Read the script 3 times.** First time reading aloud. Second time without looking at the script. Third time with a timer.

2. **The key moment is slide 10** — "best lab performance gives worst deployed performance." Pause after saying it. Let it sink in. That's the whole point of the talk.

3. **Know the numbers cold:** 515 Hz real, 361 Hz fake, 154 Hz gap, 1.32 effect size, 18% error with 1 feature, 21% with 15, 55% collapse with 36.

4. **If you go long,** cut slides 6 (redundancy) and 7 (physics). The audience can ask about those in Q&A.

5. **If you go short,** spend more time on slides 8 (codecs) and 10 (collapse) — those are the most interesting findings.

6. **For Q&A:** if you don't know the answer, say "That's a great question and I don't have the data to answer it right now." Never make something up.
